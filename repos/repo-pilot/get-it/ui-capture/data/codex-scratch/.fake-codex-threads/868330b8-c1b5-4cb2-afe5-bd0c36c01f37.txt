GOAL
You receive the text of one OR MORE pages from a textbook-style PDF. Each page
is delimited by a line "===== PAGE_INDEX n =====". Treat every page on its own
merits and identify, per page, the concepts that would benefit MOST from a
visual aid sitting next to the reader.

For each concept choose ONE renderer:
  • "3d"      — physical objects with meaningful 3D structure
                (organs, molecules, cells, anatomical regions, buildings,
                mechanical parts).
  • "2d-anim" — physical processes, simulations, mechanisms in motion
                (inclined plane, pendulum, blood flow path, chemical reaction
                progress, projectile trajectory, spring oscillation).
  • "formula" — equations, derivations, mathematical statements that benefit
                from a step-by-step LaTeX walkthrough.
  • "graph"   — function plots, scatter, bar charts, distributions, time
                series.
  • "2d-text" — citations, named statutes, articles, court rulings, named
                papers/sources, definitions where the visualizer should show
                an authoritative quote or summary.

RULES
1. Pick AT MOST 4 concepts per page. Quality over quantity. Skip pages with
   no good visualization candidates — simply emit no concepts for them.
   Judge each page independently, exactly as if it were the only page given.
2. For EVERY concept, set "page" to the integer from the "===== PAGE_INDEX n
   =====" marker of the page the concept appears on.
3. Each concept's "anchor" MUST be a verbatim copy of the LAST 30–80
   characters of the sentence that introduces it, taken EXACTLY from THAT
   page's text (no paraphrase). The tag pill will be planted right after
   this anchor — so the anchor string MUST appear once on its page, and the
   renderer anchors at its tail. Pick anchors that are unique on the page.
4. LANGUAGE: detect the language of each page's text and write BOTH "label"
   and "context" in that same language so they read naturally next to the
   source. Italian page → Italian outputs, English page → English outputs,
   etc. Never translate the source.
5. "label" is what shows on the pill (≤ 35 chars). Make it a short noun
   phrase the reader would skim and instantly recognise.
6. "context" gives the visualizer everything it needs to render the concept
   without re-reading the full page: include the concept name, key
   parameters mentioned in the text, and the field of study. 1–3 sentences.
7. Avoid trivial picks (page numbers, headings, generic phrases). Avoid
   duplicates of the same concept across a page.

OUTPUT
A single JSON object conforming to the supplied schema. No prose.

===== PAGE_INDEX 0 =====
Biochemistry — Enzyme Kinetics
Why enzymes matter
Enzymes are biological catalysts, almost always proteins, that speed up reactions by factors of
10^6 to 10^17 without being consumed. They do not change the equilibrium constant of a
reaction: they lower the   activation energy   so that equilibrium is reached faster. The substrate
binds at the   active site , a small pocket whose shape and chemistry complement the transition
state (induced-fit model, Koshland 1958).
The Michaelis-Menten model
Consider the scheme E + S <-> ES -> E + P. Assuming the concentration of the ES complex
stays roughly constant during the measurement (the   steady-state assumption , Briggs and
Haldane), the initial velocity is
v0 = Vmax [S] / (Km + [S])
•   Vmax   is the maximum rate, reached when every enzyme molecule is saturated with
substrate. Vmax = kcat [E]total.
•   Km , the Michaelis constant, is the substrate concentration at which v0 = Vmax / 2. A low
Km means the enzyme reaches half-saturation at low substrate concentration, which is
often read (loosely) as high affinity.
•   kcat , the turnover number, is how many substrate molecules one active site converts per
second. Carbonic anhydrase reaches about 10^6 per second.
•   kcat / Km   is the specificity constant. Its upper limit, around 10^8 to 10^9 per molar per
second, is set by diffusion; enzymes near it are called catalytically perfect (e.g. triose
phosphate isomerase).
The curve of v0 against [S] is a rectangular hyperbola: linear at low [S] (first-order kinetics) and
flat at high [S] (zero-order kinetics).
The Lineweaver-Burk plot
Taking the reciprocal of both sides gives a straight line:
1/v0 = (Km / Vmax)(1/[S]) + 1/Vmax
The y-intercept is 1/Vmax, the x-intercept is -1/Km, and the slope is Km/Vmax. The double-
reciprocal plot is convenient for reading inhibition patterns but distorts the error at low substrate
concentrations, so modern labs fit the hyperbola directly with non-linear regression.

A   competitive inhibitor   resembles the substrate and can be outcompeted by adding more
substrate; on a Lineweaver-Burk plot the lines cross on the y-axis. Methanol poisoning is
treated by giving ethanol, which competes for alcohol dehydrogenase. Statins are competitive
inhibitors of HMG-CoA reductase.
Irreversible inhibitors   form covalent bonds with the enzyme. Aspirin acetylates a serine in
cyclooxygenase; penicillin acylates the transpeptidase that builds bacterial cell walls.
Allosteric regulation
Allosteric enzymes have multiple subunits and give a   sigmoidal   v0 vs [S] curve instead of a
hyperbola, because binding at one site changes the affinity of the others (cooperativity).
Phosphofructokinase-1, the main control point of glycolysis, is inhibited by ATP and citrate and
activated by AMP and fructose-2,6-bisphosphate. Feedback inhibition — where the end product
of a pathway inhibits the first committed step — keeps metabolic pathways from overproducing.
Effect of temperature and pH
Rate increases with temperature until the protein begins to denature (most human enzymes
peak near 37 C). Each enzyme has an optimal pH: pepsin works best around pH 2, trypsin
around pH 8.
