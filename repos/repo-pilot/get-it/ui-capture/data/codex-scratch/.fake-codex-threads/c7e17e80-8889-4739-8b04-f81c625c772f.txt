GOAL
You receive the text of one OR MORE pages from a textbook-style PDF. Each page
is delimited by a line "===== PAGE_INDEX n =====". Treat every page on its own
merits and identify, per page, the concepts that would benefit MOST from a
visual aid sitting next to the reader.

For each concept choose ONE renderer:
  • "3d"      — physical objects with meaningful 3D structure
                (organs, molecules, cells, anatomical regions, buildings,
                mechanical parts).
  • "2d-anim" — physical processes, simulations, mechanisms in motion
                (inclined plane, pendulum, blood flow path, chemical reaction
                progress, projectile trajectory, spring oscillation).
  • "formula" — equations, derivations, mathematical statements that benefit
                from a step-by-step LaTeX walkthrough.
  • "graph"   — function plots, scatter, bar charts, distributions, time
                series.
  • "2d-text" — citations, named statutes, articles, court rulings, named
                papers/sources, definitions where the visualizer should show
                an authoritative quote or summary.

RULES
1. Pick AT MOST 4 concepts per page. Quality over quantity. Skip pages with
   no good visualization candidates — simply emit no concepts for them.
   Judge each page independently, exactly as if it were the only page given.
2. For EVERY concept, set "page" to the integer from the "===== PAGE_INDEX n
   =====" marker of the page the concept appears on.
3. Each concept's "anchor" MUST be a verbatim copy of the LAST 30–80
   characters of the sentence that introduces it, taken EXACTLY from THAT
   page's text (no paraphrase). The tag pill will be planted right after
   this anchor — so the anchor string MUST appear once on its page, and the
   renderer anchors at its tail. Pick anchors that are unique on the page.
4. LANGUAGE: detect the language of each page's text and write BOTH "label"
   and "context" in that same language so they read naturally next to the
   source. Italian page → Italian outputs, English page → English outputs,
   etc. Never translate the source.
5. "label" is what shows on the pill (≤ 35 chars). Make it a short noun
   phrase the reader would skim and instantly recognise.
6. "context" gives the visualizer everything it needs to render the concept
   without re-reading the full page: include the concept name, key
   parameters mentioned in the text, and the field of study. 1–3 sentences.
7. Avoid trivial picks (page numbers, headings, generic phrases). Avoid
   duplicates of the same concept across a page.

OUTPUT
A single JSON object conforming to the supplied schema. No prose.

Neurophysiology — Action Potential and
Synaptic Transmission
Lecture notes, Fisiologia (Prof. Bianchi), week 6. Rewritten from slides + Guyton ch. 5.
1. Resting membrane potential
At rest the inside of a neuron sits at roughly -70 mV relative to the extracellular fluid. Two things
set this value. First, the   sodium-potassium pump   (Na+/K+-ATPase) moves 3 Na+ out and 2
K+ in for every ATP it hydrolyses, building steep concentration gradients. Second, the resting
membrane is far more permeable to K+ than to Na+, because many potassium leak channels
are open. Potassium therefore diffuses out until the electrical pull back inward balances the
chemical push outward.
The equilibrium potential of a single ion is given by the   Nernst equation : E = (RT / zF)
ln([ion]out / [ion]in). For K+ at body temperature this gives about -90 mV; for Na+ about +60 mV.
The real resting potential lies between them, much closer to E_K, and is described by the
Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz equation, which weights each ion by its permeability.
2. The action potential
When a stimulus depolarises the axon hillock past the   threshold of about -55 mV , voltage-
gated sodium channels open in a positive feedback loop. Na+ rushes in and the membrane
potential overshoots to about +30 to +40 mV within half a millisecond. This is the rising phase.
Two things then end the spike. The sodium channels inactivate (the inactivation gate swings
shut), and the slower voltage-gated potassium channels open. K+ leaves the cell and the
membrane repolarises, briefly undershooting to around -80 mV: the   after-hyperpolarisation .
The action potential is   all-or-none : once threshold is crossed, the amplitude does not depend
on how strong the stimulus was. Intensity is encoded instead in the   frequency   of firing.
Refractory periods
During the absolute refractory period no second spike can be generated, because the sodium
channels are still inactivated. During the relative refractory period a spike is possible but needs
a stronger stimulus. Refractoriness is what makes propagation one-directional along the axon.
3. Propagation and myelin
In unmyelinated fibres the depolarisation spreads to the neighbouring patch of membrane and
regenerates the spike continuously, which is slow (0.5-2 m/s). In myelinated fibres the Schwann
cells (PNS) or oligodendrocytes (CNS) wrap the axon in an insulating sheath, and voltage-
gated channels cluster at the   nodes of Ranvier . The spike jumps from node to node —

===== PAGE_INDEX 1 =====
saltatory conduction   — reaching up to 120 m/s. Demyelinating diseases such as multiple
sclerosis slow or block this conduction.
Conduction velocity also increases with axon diameter, because a wider axon has lower
internal resistance (length constant grows with the square root of diameter).
4. The chemical synapse
When the spike reaches the presynaptic terminal, voltage-gated Ca2+ channels open. The
calcium influx triggers SNARE-mediated fusion of synaptic vesicles with the membrane and the
release of neurotransmitter into the cleft (about 20 nm wide). This step accounts for most of the
synaptic delay of roughly 0.5 ms .
The transmitter binds receptors on the postsynaptic membrane:
•   Ionotropic receptors are ligand-gated channels: fast (ms). Example: nicotinic ACh receptor
at the neuromuscular junction, AMPA receptors for glutamate.
•   Metabotropic receptors act through G-proteins and second messengers: slower but longer
lasting. Example: muscarinic ACh receptors in the heart.
An   EPSP   (excitatory postsynaptic potential) depolarises the cell, an   IPSP   hyperpolarises it,
typically via Cl- channels opened by GABA or glycine. A single EPSP is small (about 0.5 mV);
the neuron fires only when   temporal and spatial summation   of many inputs at the axon
hillock pushes it past threshold.
5. Termination of the signal
The signal must be switched off quickly. Acetylcholine is hydrolysed by acetylcholinesterase in
the cleft; monoamines like serotonin and dopamine are taken back up by transporters (the
target of SSRIs). Nerve agents and some insecticides block acetylcholinesterase, which causes
continuous stimulation and paralysis.
Exam tip from Bianchi: be able to draw the AP and label which channel is responsible for each
phase. He asked this two years in a row.
