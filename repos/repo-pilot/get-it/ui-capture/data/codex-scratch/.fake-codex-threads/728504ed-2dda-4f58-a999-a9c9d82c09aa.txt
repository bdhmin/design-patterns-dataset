Signals and Systems — Lecture 12: The
Fourier Transform
From series to transform
A periodic signal with period T can be written as a   Fourier series : a sum of complex
exponentials at integer multiples of the fundamental frequency f0 = 1/T. As T grows to infinity
the spacing between harmonics shrinks to zero and the sum becomes an integral. That integral
is the   continuous-time Fourier transform :
X(f) = integral of x(t) e^(-j 2 pi f t) dt, over all t
and the inverse transform rebuilds the signal: x(t) = integral of X(f) e^(j 2 pi f t) df.
X(f) is in general complex. Its magnitude |X(f)| tells us how much of each frequency is present
(the amplitude spectrum) and its angle tells us the phase.
Key transform pairs
•   A rectangular pulse of width T in time transforms to a   sinc function , T sinc(fT), in
frequency. Narrow pulse, wide spectrum.
•   A Gaussian transforms to a Gaussian — the only shape that keeps its form.
•   A pure cosine at f0 transforms to two impulses at +f0 and -f0.
•   The Dirac delta in time has a flat spectrum: it contains every frequency equally.
Properties we use constantly
1.   Linearity : the transform of a sum is the sum of the transforms.
2.   Time shift : delaying x(t) by t0 multiplies X(f) by e^(-j 2 pi f t0). Only the phase changes,
never the magnitude.
3.   Scaling : compressing a signal in time stretches its spectrum in frequency (time-bandwidth
trade-off). This is the signal-processing version of the uncertainty principle.
4.   Convolution theorem : convolution in time becomes multiplication in frequency. This is why
we analyse LTI systems in the frequency domain: Y(f) = H(f) X(f), where H(f) is the
frequency response of the system.
5.   Parseval's theorem : the energy is the same in both domains.
Sampling and aliasing
To store a signal digitally we sample it every Ts seconds. Sampling in time makes the spectrum
periodic in frequency, with copies spaced by the sampling frequency fs = 1/Ts. If the signal is

band-limited to B Hz and fs > 2B — the   Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem   — the copies
do not overlap and the original can be recovered with an ideal low-pass filter.
If fs is too low the copies overlap and high frequencies fold back as false low frequencies:
aliasing . This is why wagon wheels seem to spin backwards in films and why audio is sampled
at 44.1 kHz (just above twice the 20 kHz limit of human hearing). In practice an anti-aliasing
filter is placed before the ADC.
Application: ECG filtering
In the biomedical signals lab we record a single-lead ECG at fs = 500 Hz. Its spectrum shows
the QRS energy between roughly 5 and 40 Hz, baseline wander below 0.5 Hz from breathing,
and a sharp peak at 50 Hz from the mains. A band-pass filter (0.5-40 Hz) plus a notch at 50 Hz
cleans the trace without distorting the QRS complex.
Discrete Fourier Transform
On a computer we use the DFT of N samples, computed with the   FFT   in O(N log N) operations
instead of O(N^2) (Cooley and Tukey, 1965). Frequency resolution is fs / N: longer recordings
give finer resolution. Windowing (Hann, Hamming) reduces spectral leakage caused by cutting
the signal abruptly.
